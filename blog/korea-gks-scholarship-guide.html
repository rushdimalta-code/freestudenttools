Global Korea Scholarship (GKS) 2026 — The Complete Guide to Getting In

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 10 min read 💰 ~$50,000 value 🌍 155 countries eligible

GKS selects more than 2,000 students every year from 155 countries. That makes it one of the most generous fully funded scholarship programmes in the world — and one of the least talked about in Western circles. If you're applying for the first time, you're already late to the conversation.

The problem with most GKS guides is that they list the documents and deadlines but don't tell you what actually separates the successful applications from the 98% that don't make it. This guide does. FreeStudentTools pulled together everything you need: the real eligibility traps, both application tracks compared, what the selection panel scores, and what genuine awardees say made the difference.

What GKS actually covers

The full package for a master's student typically includes:

  • Full tuition fees at your Korean university — no cap
  • Monthly stipend of ₩1,000,000 (~$730 USD) for master's and PhD students; ₩800,000 for undergraduates
  • Round-trip economy airfare from your home country to Korea
  • Settlement allowance of ₩200,000 on arrival
  • Korean language training — one full year before your degree begins, fully paid
  • Medical insurance throughout the programme
  • Dissertation research grant of ₩210,000 once, for thesis research expenses
  • Korean language proficiency grant of ₩100,000/month if you achieve TOPIK level 5 or 6 during the programme

For a two-year master's, the combined value typically sits around $40,000–$55,000 USD, depending on your university's tuition and your lifestyle. Seoul is not cheap — but the stipend covers rent and food if you're careful.

One thing most guides skip: The one-year Korean language training isn't optional. You attend it before your degree starts, so a two-year master's actually becomes a three-year commitment in Korea. Factor that into your planning.

Are you actually eligible? The traps people miss

The surface-level eligibility looks straightforward — citizen of a GKS-designated country, under 40, degree in hand. But the fine print catches a lot of people:

  • Korean citizenship at any point: If you've ever held Korean citizenship or are a Korean national living abroad, you're ineligible regardless of your current passport.
  • Age cutoff: You must be under 40 as of the scholarship start year. Some embassies interpret this strictly as of the application deadline — check yours.
  • Health requirements: You need a medical certificate proving you're free from specific infectious diseases. Serious chronic conditions may affect eligibility — the programme has medical screening.
  • Previous GKS recipients: If you've previously held a GKS (or KGSP) scholarship, you cannot apply again at the same degree level.
  • Prior Korean study: Students currently enrolled in a Korean university are not eligible for the University Track. Embassy Track has different rules — check with your local embassy.
  • GPA minimum: 80% cumulative average (equivalent to a 2.64 on a 4.0 scale). Competitive applicants typically score 3.2+.
Country lists change: GKS revises its eligible country list occasionally. A few countries that were eligible in 2024 are not in 2026. Verify your country's status on the official Study in Korea website before you spend time applying.

Embassy Track vs University Track — which is right for you?

This is where most first-time applicants go wrong. GKS has two completely separate pathways. You apply to one, not both simultaneously.

Embassy
TrackApply via Korean Embassy in your home country
University
TrackApply directly to a Korean university

Embassy Track

You submit your application to the Korean Embassy in your country. The embassy screens, nominates candidates, and forwards them to NIIED (the National Institute for International Education, which runs GKS). If nominated, you then rank up to 3 university and programme preferences. NIIED makes the final placement.

Pros: You don't need to contact universities yourself. NIIED places you based on your preferences and availability.

Cons: You don't choose the university — you state preferences, but NIIED's final decision is binding. High competition varies wildly by country. Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt, India, and Bangladesh are among the most competitive Embassy Track countries.

University Track

You apply directly to specific Korean universities that are GKS-partner institutions. The university runs its own screening and nominates you to NIIED. Around 57 universities participate in University Track.

Pros: You know exactly which university and programme you're going for. Professor relationships matter — contacting a potential supervisor before applying can significantly improve your odds.

Cons: You must research and contact universities yourself. Deadlines vary (typically March–April). Some universities only offer limited GKS places — check their websites directly.

Which track is better? If you have a specific university or research supervisor in mind, University Track. If you're flexible on placement, Embassy Track lets NIIED match you — and countries with lower application volumes from the 155-country list can have acceptance rates of 5–10% through Embassy Track.

What the selection panel actually scores

NIIED publishes a scoring rubric, and most applicants ignore it. Here's what's actually evaluated:

  • Academic record (40 points): GPA transcript. There's a formula — scoring maxes out around 3.8/4.0 equivalent. The floor is 80%, but competitive applicants are 90%+.
  • Study plan (20 points): Your statement of purpose — why Korea, why this university, why this research direction. This is where most applications lose points. Vague plans and generic "I want to learn Korean culture" statements score low.
  • Recommendation letters (15 points): Two letters required. A lukewarm letter from a famous professor beats a glowing letter from someone with no academic standing — but only barely. Specific examples of your capability outperform generic praise.
  • Essay on Korea (5 points): A short essay on your impressions of Korea or how you'll contribute to Korea-home country relations. Don't skip this — 5 points matter when the field is tight.
  • Interview (20 points): Not all embassies interview. Those that do ask about your study plan, research motivation, and post-degree plans. Saying you'll use your degree to strengthen ties between your country and Korea scores well with selection committees.

How to write a study plan that scores 18+ out of 20

The study plan is the part of the GKS application that most people write in a day. That's the mistake. It's worth 20 points — the same weight as your interview — and it's read by people who read hundreds of applications from your country.

A low-scoring study plan sounds like: "I want to study in Korea because Korean technology is advanced and I admire Korean culture. I will work hard to learn."

A high-scoring study plan does four things:

  1. Names a specific research problem — not a field, a problem. "Improving rural water access through low-cost filtration membranes" beats "studying environmental engineering."
  2. Connects your existing academic work to that problem — what have you already done that's relevant? What gap are you filling?
  3. Names a specific professor or lab at your target university — and explains why their work specifically connects to yours. This requires actual research before you apply.
  4. States what you'll do after returning home — Korea invests in GKS students with the expectation of bilateral benefit. Show that clearly.
The Korea connection matters: Reviewers score higher when applicants demonstrate genuine knowledge of Korean academia, not just Korean pop culture. If you can cite a specific paper published by Korean researchers in your field, that's noticed.

Getting strong recommendation letters

Two letters are required. Most applicants ask professors they know well and hope for the best. Here's how to actually get useful letters:

Brief your recommender on exactly what GKS is looking for. Give them your study plan so they can reference specific projects in their letter. Ask them to address your potential for independent research — that's what GKS cares about at graduate level, not your ability to pass exams.

If one of your recommenders has published research in Korea, collaborated with Korean institutions, or attended Korean conferences, mention that context in their letter. It signals existing ties.

The document checklist — what trips people up

  • Apostille on your degree certificate: Required in most cases. This can take 2–6 weeks in many countries. Start early.
  • Translated documents: All documents not in Korean or English must be officially translated. Self-translation is not accepted.
  • Medical certificate: Must be issued by a licensed hospital within 3 months of the application deadline. Don't get it done too early.
  • Passport validity: Your passport must be valid well beyond the scholarship start date. Renew it before applying if needed.
  • GPA conversion: If your university doesn't grade on a 4.0 scale, you'll need to calculate the equivalent. NIIED uses a specific conversion formula — check the official guidelines, not a generic GPA converter.
Common rejection reasons: Incomplete documents (one missing apostille ends your application), expired passport, applying from an ineligible country, and study plans that read like a Wikipedia article on Korea rather than a research proposal.

What happens after you arrive in Korea

If you're accepted, the first year isn't your degree — it's Korean language training. You'll study Korean full-time at a designated language institution. This is non-negotiable, even if you already speak Korean.

After language training, you start your actual degree. During this period, the monthly stipend continues. If you achieve TOPIK 5 or 6 (the top two Korean language proficiency levels), you get an additional ₩100,000/month for the rest of your programme. Many students do reach this — Korean immersion for a year is intensive.

You're required to return to your home country after the scholarship ends. GKS isn't a pathway to Korean permanent residency — it's explicitly designed as an exchange investment. Students who plan to stay in Korea after graduation through other visa routes need to plan that independently.

What if GKS doesn't work out?

Korea also offers several university-specific scholarships outside GKS. POSTECH, KAIST, and SNU all have independent international student scholarships. Some are smaller (partial funding), but the competition is lower and you can apply to them and GKS simultaneously via different tracks.

The realistic application timeline

  • October–November (year before): Research universities, contact potential supervisors (University Track), check your country's embassy announcement
  • December–January: Draft your study plan, request recommendation letters (allow 4–6 weeks), begin apostille and translation process
  • January–February: Embassy Track applications typically open and close. Check your embassy — deadlines vary by country
  • March–April: University Track deadlines (varies by university)
  • March–May: Interviews (Embassy Track, where applicable)
  • June–August: Results announced
  • February (following year): Korean language training begins
  • March (year after): Degree programme starts
The real lesson from successful GKS applicants: They didn't start two weeks before the deadline. The study plan, letters, apostilles, and medical certificate each need time. Six months of lead time is not paranoid — it's the norm among people who actually get in.

The bottom line

GKS is one of the best-funded, most accessible fully funded scholarships for students from the Global South. More than 155 countries are eligible. The package is genuinely comprehensive. And unlike some scholarships, there's no financial need requirement — it's academic merit and potential.

The reason most people don't get in isn't weak academics. It's vague study plans, rushed document preparation, or simply not knowing the programme well enough to present a compelling case. Now you know what the committee is scoring. Use it.

Start with the official Study in Korea website for the current country list and application portal, and contact your local Korean Embassy for your country's specific Embassy Track dates.
