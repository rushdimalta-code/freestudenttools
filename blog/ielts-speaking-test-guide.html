IELTS Speaking Test: How to Prepare for Parts 1, 2 and 3

📅 August 2026 ⏱ 10 min read ✍️ FreeStudentTools

Most people fail the IELTS Speaking test before they open their mouth. They've spent weeks memorising vocabulary lists and rehearsing "perfect" sentences, then freeze when the examiner goes slightly off-script. The fix isn't more vocabulary — it's understanding what the examiner is actually scoring and training your mouth for real conversation, not rehearsed speeches.

FreeStudentTools has put together this guide covering all three parts, the four scoring criteria, and what a 4-week preparation plan looks like that actually works.

How does the IELTS Speaking test work?

The Speaking test is a face-to-face interview with a certified IELTS examiner, lasting 11–14 minutes total. It takes place either on the same day as your other tests or a few days before or after, depending on your test centre. Unlike Reading or Listening, you can't pause it, go back, or take extra time.

Part 1 Introduction and Interview — 4–5 minutes

The examiner asks about you: where you're from, your work or studies, your hobbies, your home. Topics are familiar — family, food, travel, technology, free time. The point is to ease you in and start collecting evidence on your fluency. Don't give one-word answers, but don't over-explain either. Two to three sentences per answer is right.

Part 2 Long Turn — 3–4 minutes

You get a task card with a topic and three or four bullet points. You have exactly 1 minute to prepare (pencil and paper provided), then you speak for up to 2 minutes. Topics: "Describe a person who has influenced you" or "Talk about a place you found interesting." Part 2 catches candidates who've never practised speaking for a sustained 2 minutes — it feels much longer than it sounds.

Part 3 Two-Way Discussion — 4–5 minutes

The examiner extends the Part 2 topic into a broader discussion. If Part 2 was about someone who influenced you, Part 3 might be: "Do you think famous people have more influence on young people today than in the past?" This is where Band 7+ scores are won or lost. The examiner wants opinions, arguments, and the ability to handle abstract ideas — not just personal anecdotes.

What do examiners actually score?

Four criteria, each worth 25% of your Speaking band:

CriterionWhat it meansWhat kills your score
Fluency & CoherenceSpeaking at a natural pace without long pauses; ideas that connect logicallyLong silences, repeating yourself, starting sentences you can't finish
Lexical ResourceRange and accuracy of vocabulary; topic-specific words used naturallyRepeating the same words, very basic vocabulary, words used incorrectly
Grammatical Range & AccuracyVariety of sentence structures, control of tense, accurate grammarOnly simple sentences, consistent tense errors
PronunciationBeing consistently intelligible; appropriate stress and intonationBeing hard to follow, mispronouncing common words, flat robotic delivery
The accent myth: You don't need a British or American accent to score Band 7 or 8 in Pronunciation. The criterion is about intelligibility, not origin. Many Band 8 and 9 speakers have strong non-native accents. The examiner listens for whether stress falls in the right place and whether your speech is consistently clear.

How should you prepare for Part 1?

Part 1 questions come from a short list of predictable topics: work, studies, hometown, hobbies, sports, reading, music, technology, food, weather, travel, family, shopping. You don't know which will come up, but you can prepare for all of them.

The biggest Part 1 mistake is answering too briefly. "Yes, I like reading" is not an answer — it's a sentence fragment. Extend it: "Yes, I've always enjoyed reading. I mostly read non-fiction — business and history — though I've been trying to read more fiction lately because I think it helps with vocabulary." That's three connected ideas, natural detail, and higher vocabulary range. Same content, completely different score.

  • Record yourself answering 5 Part 1 questions every day. Play it back. Note where you pause, stumble, or repeat words.
  • Don't memorise scripts. Memorise vocabulary clusters for each topic — 8–10 words per topic — then speak freely.
  • Add "why" to every answer. If you say you enjoy something, explain why. "Why" creates coherence automatically.

How should you prepare for Part 2?

Part 2 is where even fluent English speakers underperform because they've never practised speaking for 2 uninterrupted minutes in their life. Candidates either run dry at the 45-second mark or keep circling back to the same points and lose coherence.

Use the 1-minute preparation time properly. Don't try to write sentences — you won't be able to read and speak simultaneously. Note 4–5 keywords or short phrases mapping to the bullet points on the card, then add one or two personal details or a specific memory.

The 4-point structure that works for almost any Part 2 topic: (1) What/Who/Where — give the basic description. (2) When — add a time reference. (3) How it made you feel or why it was significant. (4) What you learned or how it's relevant now. This takes roughly 90 seconds minimum if you develop each point. Add a short conclusion and you're at 2 minutes.

Practise Part 2 every day with a timer. Pull a random topic from any IELTS practice book, set 1 minute to prepare, then speak for exactly 2 minutes. Record it. If you finished early, identify where you could have added more detail. If you rambled, practise cutting to the point faster.

How should you prepare for Part 3?

Part 3 is where your score separates from other candidates. Questions are abstract — about society, trends, change over time — and most test-takers either give vague one-sentence answers or lose grammar under pressure.

The examiner wants a developed position. If the question is "Do you think people are more or less creative today than in the past?", try: "I think it's complicated — people have more tools to express creativity now, but I'm not sure the underlying creative thinking has changed. If anything, the pressure to constantly produce content might actually reduce the time people spend on deep creative work." That acknowledges complexity, uses hedging language, and develops a specific idea rather than restating the question — a Band 7+ answer.

Useful phrases for Part 3

  • Giving an opinion: "From my perspective…", "I'd argue that…", "It seems to me that…"
  • Acknowledging complexity: "It depends on the context, but…", "There are different sides — on one hand…"
  • Buying thinking time: "That's an interesting question — let me think…", "I've never really considered that, but I suppose…"
  • Comparing past and present: "Traditionally… whereas now…", "The shift in recent years has been toward…"

What score do you need, and what does each band require?

Most UK, Australian, and Canadian universities require an overall IELTS band of 6.5–7.0, with no individual section below 6.0 or 6.5. Some postgraduate programmes require a minimum Speaking band of 7.0 regardless of overall score.

BandWhat it sounds likeWhat to fix
5.0–5.5Frequent pauses, limited vocabulary, grammar errors affecting meaningBuild basic fluency — speak English daily; reduce response latency
6.0–6.5Generally understandable, some pauses, errors but meaning is clearExpand topic vocabulary, reduce repetition, develop Part 2 and 3 answers more fully
7.0–7.5Fluent with occasional errors, good vocabulary range, some grammatical complexityReduce mid-sentence pauses, vary sentence structure, use hedging in Part 3
8.0+Natural, flexible, very rare errors, wide vocabulary and grammatical rangeMinor refinements to pronunciation precision

What does a 4-week IELTS Speaking preparation plan look like?

Four weeks is enough to meaningfully improve your Speaking band — but only if you practise speaking, not just studying about speaking. The mistake most candidates make is spending all their time reading grammar guides and vocabulary lists without opening their mouths.

  • Week 1: Record yourself answering 10 Part 1 questions each day. Don't correct yourself mid-sentence. Get comfortable hearing your voice and identifying your weakest area.
  • Week 2: Focus on Part 2. Do one timed 2-minute response daily. Build a vocabulary bank of 8–10 words per topic area (hobbies, technology, environment, health, education).
  • Week 3: Add Part 3. Watch one English-language news segment per day and record your opinion on the topic in Part 3 style. Practise hedging language and complex sentence structures.
  • Week 4: Full mock tests — all three parts, timed, recorded. Find a speaking partner or tutor for real feedback, since your own ear misses things a listener catches.
Don't over-rehearse set answers. IELTS examiners are trained to spot memorised responses. If your answer sounds scripted, they'll deviate from the question to test whether you can actually speak flexibly. A slightly imperfect but natural answer scores higher than a polished monologue that breaks down the moment the examiner asks a follow-up.

What should you do if you don't understand a question?

Ask for clarification — "Could you repeat that, please?" or "Sorry, I didn't quite catch that — do you mean…?" is 100% acceptable and will not reduce your score. What will reduce your score is going silent for 5 seconds, then giving an answer to a question you didn't understand. Examiners expect this. They're not trying to trick you.

If you're unsure what a word means in a Part 3 question, it's also fine to say "I'm not certain I understand the word — do you mean something like…?" — that itself demonstrates active language competence.
