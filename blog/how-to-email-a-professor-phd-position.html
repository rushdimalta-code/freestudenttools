How to Email a Professor for a PhD Position — and What a Reply Actually Gets You

📅 August 2026 ⏱ 9 min read 📧 ~10-25% reply rate on cold emails

A professor at a research university gets somewhere between ten and forty of these emails a week during application season. Most open with "Dear Sir/Madam, I am writing to express my strong interest in pursuing a PhD under your esteemed supervision." Most get deleted in the time it takes to read that sentence.

The emails that get a reply do one thing the others don't: they prove, in the first two lines, that you read something the professor actually wrote and had a specific thought about it. Everything else in the email is admin. This guide covers when the email is worth sending at all, what goes in it, and the part everyone skips.

Why do most emails to professors get ignored?

Three reasons, in order of how common they are:

  • It's obviously a template. "Your research on [topic] aligns with my interests" with a comma splice where the mail-merge field didn't fill. Professors have seen thousands of these.
  • It asks for something the professor can't give in an email. "Please tell me if you will accept me as your PhD student" — nobody decides that from a cold email. The realistic ask is smaller: a five-minute reply about whether they're taking students at all.
  • There's no reason to reply now. No question, no deadline, nothing that makes ignoring it feel like a loss. A polite email with no hook goes to the "later" pile and stays there.
The counterintuitive part: a shorter email works better. A 150-word message with one sharp question about a recent paper gets answered more often than a 500-word email with your full academic history attached in the body. The CV goes in the attachment; the email is the argument for opening it.

When does emailing a professor actually matter?

Whether you need to email at all depends entirely on the system you're applying into. Getting this wrong wastes weeks.

SystemDoes emailing a professor matter?
United StatesYou apply to the department, not a person. Admissions committees decide. Emailing helps at the margin — a professor flagging your file as "I'd take this student" carries weight — but a cold email is not required and rarely decisive. Apply first, connect second.
UKOften you need a supervisor to informally agree to take you before you submit a formal application, especially for self-proposed projects. Advertised, funded studentships work more like the US. The email matters here.
Europe (Germany, Netherlands, Nordics, Switzerland)Most funded PhD positions are advertised jobs with a salary and a fixed project. You apply to the posting. A cold email about an unadvertised position is usually a dead end unless the professor has grant money and no posting yet.
Australia / New ZealandSimilar to the UK — you generally need a supervisor to agree in principle before the scholarship application goes in.

If you're targeting the US, spend your energy on the application and use email sparingly to ask genuinely open questions (is the lab taking students, is a specific project still active). If you're targeting the UK, Australia, or an unadvertised European position, the email is a required step and worth getting right.

What should you put in the email?

Five parts, in this order, roughly 150 words total:

  1. One line: who you are and what you want. "I'm a final-year materials science student at [University] applying for PhD positions starting autumn 2027."
  2. Two-three lines: the specific thing you read and your thought about it. Not "your work on batteries is interesting." Instead: "Your 2025 paper on sulfide electrolyte grain boundaries argued that the interfacial resistance is dominated by [X]. I've been working on [related thing] and I'm curious whether [specific question]." This is the part that gets a reply.
  3. One-two lines: what you'd bring. A concrete skill or result. "I spent a year running electrochemical impedance spectroscopy in [lab] and co-authored [paper/preprint]." Keep it to what's relevant to their work.
  4. The ask — small and answerable. "Are you planning to take PhD students for the 2027 intake? If so, I'd value a short call to discuss fit before I apply." Or, for a job posting: "Is the position advertised in [link] still open, and would a background in [X] be a fit?"
  5. Sign-off with attachments named. "I've attached my CV and transcript. Thank you for your time." Two attachments, PDF, sensibly named: Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf.
Subject line: make it specific and skimmable. "Prospective PhD student — grain boundary resistance in sulfide electrolytes (2027 intake)" beats "PhD Inquiry" every time. The professor should know what the email is about before opening it.

The paper you have to read

This is the step people skip, and it's the whole game. Before you email, read one of the professor's recent papers — ideally from the last two years — properly. Not the abstract. The methods and the discussion.

You're looking for one of three things to react to: a limitation the authors acknowledge, a result that surprised you, or a method you'd apply differently. Your email then contains a sentence only someone who read that paper could write. A professor can tell the difference between "I read your abstract and pattern-matched keywords" and "I read your paper and thought about it" in about four seconds.

If you genuinely can't find something specific to say after reading a paper, that's useful information: this might not be the right lab for you, and a generic email won't fix that.

When should you send it?

  • Best: 3-6 months before the application deadline. Early enough that the professor has bandwidth, late enough that intake plans exist.
  • Avoid: the two-three weeks right before a major admissions deadline, when the professor is buried in files. Your email joins the pile.
  • Also avoid: mid-August and late December — low reply rates everywhere.
  • Weekday mornings (their time zone) get opened more than Friday afternoons.

Following up

No reply after 10-14 working days? Send one follow-up: a two-line reply to your own email, "Following up on the note below in case it's helpful — happy to send more detail on [X]." One follow-up, then stop. A second or third follow-up reads as pushy and won't change the outcome. Silence usually means "not taking students" or "not a fit," and that's a normal answer, not a personal one.

Two templates

Cold email, UK / self-proposed project:

Subject: Prospective PhD student — [specific topic] (2027 intake)

Dear Professor [Name],

I'm a final-year [subject] student at [University], applying for PhD positions starting autumn 2027. I read your [year] paper on [specific topic] — the point about [specific finding or limitation] stood out to me, because in my final-year project on [related thing] I found [brief, specific observation]. I'd be interested in whether [one clear question].

My background is in [one or two relevant skills/results]. Are you planning to take PhD students for the 2027 intake? If so, I'd value a short call to discuss fit before submitting a formal application.

I've attached my CV and transcript. Thank you for your time.

Best regards,
[Full name]

Reply to an advertised position:

Subject: Application query — PhD position [reference number], [project title]

Dear Professor [Name],

I'd like to apply for the PhD position advertised at [link / reference]. My background is in [X], including [one concrete result or skill directly relevant to the project]. I read the linked project description and [the associated paper]; I'm particularly interested in [specific element of the project].

Before I submit the formal application, could you confirm the position is still open and whether experience in [X rather than Y] would be a fit? CV and transcript attached.

Thank you,
[Full name]

What a "yes" actually means

A positive reply almost never means "you're in." It means one of:

  • "I'm taking students — apply through the formal process and I'll support your file." (US, and advertised UK studentships.)
  • "I'd supervise you — now we need to find funding." (UK/Australia self-proposed. This is a real step forward but funding is a separate, hard problem.)
  • "Let's have a call." (Everywhere. The call is the actual interview. Prepare for it like one.)

None of those is an offer. The offer comes from the university after the formal application, the funding decision, and usually an interview. The email's job is only to get you into that process with someone already on your side. Send five good ones to labs you'd genuinely want to join, not fifty identical ones. The five will out-perform the fifty.
