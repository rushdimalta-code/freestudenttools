Proof of Funds for a Student Visa: How Much, and How to Show It

📅 August 2026 ⏱ 10 min read 📅 UK: 28 consecutive days of held funds

Every student visa refusal statistics report says the same thing: a large share of rejections are about money, and most of those are not "you don't have enough." They're "the way you showed it doesn't meet the rule." A student with plenty of family savings gets refused because the money landed in the account nine days before applying. Someone with exactly the right balance gets refused because the statement was two months old.

This guide gives the current figures for the four biggest destinations, then the rules that actually cause problems: how long the money has to sit there, which sources count, and what a sponsor has to provide.

What does "proof of funds" actually mean?

You're proving two things: that the money exists and that it is available to you for tuition and living costs. Officers are trained to spot money that was borrowed or moved in temporarily to pass the check — "parked funds." A stable balance you can explain beats a large balance that appeared from nowhere.

The amount you need is almost always: one year of tuition + a set living-cost figure + (sometimes) travel, minus anything already paid (a tuition deposit, a scholarship, an accommodation payment) if you have receipts.

United Kingdom (Student visa)

  • Tuition: your first year's fee, as stated on your CAS, minus any deposit already paid.
  • Living costs: £1,023 per month if your course is in London, £820 per month outside London, for up to 9 months (so £9,207 or £7,380 maximum).
  • Holding period: the money must sit in the account for 28 consecutive days, and the closing balance must never dip below the required total during those 28 days.
  • Statement age: your evidence must be dated no more than 31 days before you apply.
  • You're exempt from showing living-cost funds if you've been in the UK with a valid visa for 12 months or more at the date of application.
The 28-day rule is the single biggest UK trap. If you move money between your own accounts, restart the 28-day clock. Plan for the money to be in one account, untouched, for at least a month before you apply.

United States (F-1)

  • Your I-20 lists the estimated cost of attendance for one year (tuition + fees + living + insurance). You must show funds covering that figure.
  • There is no fixed holding period in the regulations, but the consular officer at your visa interview will judge whether funds are genuine and available. Money deposited days before the interview invites questions.
  • Funds can come from personal savings, family, a sponsor, a loan sanction letter, or a scholarship — each needs its own document (see below).
  • You show evidence at the interview and when SEVIS/the school issues the I-20; you don't upload a bank statement to a portal the way you do for the UK.

Canada (Study permit)

  • Living costs (outside Quebec): C$20,635 per year for a single applicant, on top of first-year tuition and travel. Quebec sets its own figures.
  • The most reliable route is a Guaranteed Investment Certificate (GIC) of the required living-cost amount from a participating Canadian bank — it's specifically designed to satisfy the funds requirement and is effectively expected under the Student Direct Stream where that applies.
  • Also acceptable: bank statements for the past 4 months, a bank loan sanction letter, proof of paid tuition and housing, a Canadian bank account showing transferred money, or a scholarship letter.

Australia (Subclass 500)

  • Living costs: A$29,710 per year for the primary student (figure set in 2024), plus airfares and first-year tuition.
  • Evidence can be bank statements, a loan from a bank or government, a scholarship, or evidence that a parent/spouse earns a specified annual income (an alternative to showing a lump sum).
  • Australia applies the Genuine Student requirement to the whole application — funds that are clearly borrowed just for the visa, with no plausible repayment capacity, count against you.

What is the 28-day (seasoning) rule?

"Seasoning" means how long the money has been sitting in the account. The UK writes it into the rule (28 days). The US, Canada and Australia don't give a number but apply the same logic through officer discretion.

What this means in practice:

  • A balance that's been stable for 3-6 months is the strongest evidence. Get your statements to cover that period.
  • A large deposit in the last few weeks needs a documented explanation: a property sale deed, a matured fixed deposit certificate, a gift deed from a parent plus their statements showing the money came from their savings. "It's my uncle's money" with nothing attached is a refusal.
  • Don't consolidate accounts right before applying. If your funds are spread across three accounts, moving them into one resets the clock and can look like manufacturing a balance.

Fixed deposits / term deposits in your or a sponsor's nameOverdraft facilities or credit limits presented as "funds"
Scholarship or assistantship award letter stating the amountProperty valuations without a sale (you can't spend a house)
GIC (Canada) from a participating bankStocks/crypto shown at a volatile spot value with no liquidation

What if someone else is paying?

A sponsor — usually a parent — needs to provide, at minimum:

  • A signed sponsorship letter stating they will cover your tuition and living costs, for how long, and the total amount.
  • The sponsor's own bank statements (same seasoning expectations) and/or income proof (tax returns, salary slips).
  • Proof of relationship — birth certificate, family register, or equivalent linking you to the sponsor.

The UK allows official financial sponsors and parents/legal guardians to hold the funds; money in a sibling's or friend's account generally does not count. The US and Australia accept broader sponsorship but expect the paper trail above.

What format do the documents need to be in?

  • Statements on official bank letterhead or downloaded as an official PDF with the bank's name and your name visible — not a screenshot of an app balance.
  • Dated within the window (UK: 31 days; others: as recent as possible).
  • Show the account holder's name, account number, currency, and a running balance — not just a single figure.
  • If not in English, include a certified translation.
  • Convert to the destination currency using a defensible rate and note the date.
  • Keep originals or certified copies for the visa interview even if you also upload them.

The pattern across all four countries is the same: show a stable balance you can explain, covering tuition plus the published living figure, dated recently, from a source with a paper trail. Get the money in place two months before you plan to apply, not two weeks.
